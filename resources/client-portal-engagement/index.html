Client Portal Engagement: How Coaches Get Clients to Log In

Most clients ignore a portal unless you give them a reason to open it. Here is how coaches build client portal engagement that lasts, and how to measure it.

By Mitch Russo · Updated 2026-10-22

Client portal engagement comes down to one question: does your client have a reason to open the portal between sessions? When the portal holds their goals, this week’s action steps, their progress and their next session, they open it. When it only stores PDFs and invoices, they don’t. This guide shows how to build the first kind, how to introduce it, and how to measure whether it works.

TL;DR

  • Clients log in when the portal answers a personal question: “What am I working on, and how am I doing?”

What client portal engagement means for a coach

A client portal is a secure, branded space where a client sees their own information and does their own work. Engagement is whether they actually use it. A portal with 100% of clients invited and 20% logging in is an expensive filing cabinet.

For coaches, engagement is not about page views. It is about the work between sessions: completing homework, logging reflections, updating goals, and arriving at the next call prepared. That is where transformation happens, and it is the part of coaching you can’t see unless the client does it somewhere you can see.

There is evidence that this matters beyond coaching. In a 2023 study of 849 financial planners by eMoney Advisor and the Financial Planning Association, 80% of planners with highly engaged portal clients reported increased client trust, 74% reduced client anxiety, and 67% more referrals. The study defined “Portal Power Users” as practices where more than 40% of clients actively use the portal. It is a financial planning study, not a coaching one, but the mechanism is familiar: clients who engage between meetings feel more supported and more loyal.

Why most clients ignore the portal

Healthcare has studied this more than any other field. A review in the Annals of Internal Medicine found that 90% of US health systems offer patient portals, yet only 15% to 30% of patients use them. The authors point to login difficulty and a lack of perceived need, and they recommend usability measurement and one-on-one training.

Coaching portals fail for the same reasons:

  • No personal reason to log in. The coach says “everything’s in the portal” but the client sees nothing that is theirs.
  • Login friction. Forgotten passwords and unclear links end the habit before it starts.
  • Duplicate channels. If you also send homework by email and answer questions by text, the portal is optional, and clients treat it that way.
  • No visible response. The client logs a reflection and nothing happens. They stop.

Every fix below addresses one of these four problems.

Give clients a reason to open it

The strongest lever is relevance. The eMoney Client Portal Playbook makes the point that a portal strategy should never be one-size-fits-all: work with each client to set up the features that serve their own objectives, rather than dictating how they use it.

In a coaching practice, that looks like this:

  1. Start from the client’s goal. Put their stated goal and success measures on the first screen they see. If it is their goal, they will want to check it.
  2. Make homework live there. Every action step has a due date and a place to mark it done or add a note.
  3. Show progress visually. Scores from recurring assessments, completed actions and milestones give clients proof they are moving. It also gives you evidence for renewal conversations.
  4. Keep the logistics there too. Next session, notes you choose to share, contracts and invoices in one place mean one login habit instead of five.

The goal is a portal clients check because it helps them, not because you asked.

Onboard the portal like a session

The healthcare research found that practices with strong adoption invest in dedicated, one-on-one explanation and make sure patients are comfortable with the tool on their own devices, treating it as a complement to human interaction. Coaches can copy this almost exactly.

A 10-minute portal walkthrough

During or right after your first session:

  1. Ask the client to open the portal on the phone or laptop they will actually use.
  2. Have them log in while you watch. Fix any password or email problems on the spot.
  3. Show three things only: their goal, this week’s action steps, and where to add a note.
  4. Ask them to complete one small task together with you, such as rating where they are today on one goal.
  5. Tell them what you will do with it: “I read this before every session.”

That last line is the most important. Clients engage when they know a person reads what they write.

A first-week checklist

  • Welcome message sent with a direct link, not a generic homepage.
  • First task assigned that takes under five minutes.
  • Check on day three: did they log in? If not, a short personal message, not an automated nag.
  • Reference something they entered in the portal at your second session.

Close the loop: respond to what clients enter

A 2024 randomized experiment in behavioral weight loss tested what happens when coaches can see participants’ digital self-monitoring data. With 322 participants over 12 weeks, the group whose coaches had access lost 6.2% of body weight versus 5.3% in the comparison group, and the authors link this to a stronger sense of supportive accountability. Both groups tracked at similar rates, so the difference was not more tracking. It was that someone was looking.

This is a weight loss program with a clinical setting, so don’t promise coaching clients a specific result. The takeaway for your practice is narrower and practical: when clients know their coach reviews their data and reflects it back, accountability feels supportive rather than punitive.

A simple review routine:

  • Spend five minutes before each session reading the client’s portal activity.
  • Open the session with one specific observation: “I saw you finished three of four actions, and the one you skipped was the networking call.”
  • Reply to notes within a day or two, even briefly.

Use AI carefully, with you in charge

AI can lighten the homework-drafting load, but clients care who is behind it. Nicky Terblanche’s research on an AI chatbot coach assistant suggests that clients experience AI-augmented coaching as psychologically safe and synergetic when the human coach endorses the technology and stays the primary relationship. Clients valued the tools for goal tracking, accountability and between-session convenience.

So draft with AI if it saves time, then review, edit and assign the work in your own voice. Tell clients that you use it and that you remain the one coaching them.

Measure engagement with numbers you can act on

You don’t need a dashboard of 30 metrics. Start with five:

Metric What it tells you Review
First-week login rate Whether onboarding worked Every new client
Homework completion rate Whether tasks are relevant and sized right Weekly or per session
Action-plan updates before a session Whether clients prepare Per session
Days since last login Early sign of disengagement Weekly
Progress trend on key goals Whether engagement links to outcomes Monthly or quarterly

Use the 40% line from the FPA research as a loose benchmark, not a promise: if fewer than four in ten of your clients use the portal actively, onboarding is your first suspect.

When a number drops, change one thing at a time. Shorter tasks, a better first-session walkthrough, or a personal check-in message are all easier to evaluate than a full redesign. Keep client data in one secure place

Engagement depends on trust. Clients write candid things about their work, relationships and fears. If those notes end up scattered across email, shared docs and messaging apps, your risk grows and so does their hesitation.

NIST’s guidance on securing systems that hold sensitive data highlights protecting sensitive data, role-based access control and anomaly monitoring as core practices. That publication covers property management systems, so treat it as a general security reference, not coaching-specific rules. The principle carries over: fewer disconnected tools means fewer places for data to leak. Ask any vendor, ClickCoach included, how access is controlled and where your data lives before you move clients over. If you work with health-related information, check which regulations apply to your practice with a qualified professional.

Our perspective: the portal is part of the method

We built ClickCoach because coaches kept telling us the same thing: the sessions were great, but the in-between was chaos. Homework in one app, notes in another, invoices somewhere else, and clients unsure where to look.

Here is what we believe. A portal should not be a place you send clients to. It should be the backbone of your method between sessions: the goal, the plan, the homework, the progress and the conversation in one login. When coaches tell us clients engage, it is almost always because the portal reflects the coaching, not because of a clever feature.

We also believe that engagement should never come at the cost of the relationship. Automation can remind and draft, but the coach decides what to assign and what to say. Clients stay when they feel seen, and they can show you progress when they can see it themselves.

A 30-day plan to lift engagement

Week 1: audit. List every place clients currently receive tasks, notes and invoices. Pick one home for each and retire the rest.

Week 2: rewrite onboarding. Build the 10-minute walkthrough and the first-week checklist above. Use them with every new client.

Week 3: re-engage existing clients. Offer a short walkthrough to current clients who have never logged in. Frame it around their goal, not the software.

Week 4: review the numbers. Check the five metrics, pick the weakest one, and change one thing.

FAQ

What does a client portal do for a coach?

It gives clients a secure, branded space for their goals, homework, notes, sessions, contracts and payments. For you, it puts the between-session work in one place so you can see and respond to it. That visibility is what makes accountability work.

What does client engagement mean in coaching?

It means the client actively takes part in the work, not just the calls. That includes completing action steps, reflecting, tracking progress and arriving prepared. Portal activity is one measurable signal of it.

How do I get clients to actually use the portal?

Walk them through it on their own device in the first week, tie it to their goal, and let them see that you read what they enter. Research on patient portals found that login problems and no perceived need are the main barriers, and one-on-one training helps.
