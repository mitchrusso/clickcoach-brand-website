No-Show Fees for Coaching: Policy, Wording and Amounts

How to set a no-show and late-cancellation fee for your coaching practice: what to charge, how to word it, what the law expects, and how to enforce it kindly.

By Mitch Russo · Updated 2026-10-29

Yes, you can charge a coaching client for a no-show or a late cancellation. The fee holds up best when it is reasonable, written down, and agreed before the client books again. This guide shows you how to set the amount, word the policy, collect the fee, and handle the client who keeps missing sessions.

TL;DR

  • Put the policy in your coaching agreement and send it before the first session, not after the first miss.
  • Keep the fee reasonable. Charging the session fee, or a stated fixed fee below it, is the common approach. A charge far above the service price can be unenforceable.
  • Treat a single miss and a pattern differently. A pattern deserves a direct, curious conversation before it deserves an invoice.
  • Reminders reduce misses. Fees only cover the cost of the ones that still happen.
  • This is general information, not legal advice. Rules vary by state, so check yours with an attorney.

Why coaches need a no-show policy

A missed session costs you twice. You lose the income, and you lose the slot you could have offered to someone else. Coaching is also built on momentum. When a client skips a call, the work between sessions often stalls too.

Most coaches feel awkward raising money with a client they are trying to support. That discomfort is the real reason policies go unwritten. A clear policy fixes it. When the rule exists in writing, you are not judging the client. You are applying an agreement you both signed.

A good policy does three jobs:

  1. It sets an expectation of mutual commitment.
  2. It protects your calendar.
  3. It gives you a calm, neutral thing to point to when a miss happens.

Is it legal to charge a no-show fee as a coach?

In general, yes. FindLaw’s consumer guidance says businesses may usually charge late-cancellation or no-show fees when the fee is disclosed, while unusually high charges can be unlawful or unenforceable. Its example of a $300 charge on a $200 service is the kind of gap that causes trouble.

There is no single federal rule for coaching no-show fees, and coaching is not a licensed profession in most states. That means you are mostly governed by contract law and general consumer protection. A few things to know:

  • Disclosure matters. Texas regulates appointment fees for certain licensed professionals. Its administrative code permits a reasonable cancellation or no-show fee, but requires written notice of the exact fees, conditions and policies before a new patient schedules again. That rule is not written for coaches. It does show the logic regulators use: reasonable amount, written terms, notice up front.
  • Some states regulate certain contracts. New York’s General Business Law §394-b covers some contracts for instruction in physical or social skills. It requires specific cancellation language, a full refund within three days of receipt, and afterward allows a reasonable and fair service fee. Whether a coaching agreement falls under a rule like this depends on the program and the state.
  • Recurring billing has its own rules. If your no-show fee or your program is charged automatically on a recurring basis, the FTC has said in a complaint against a seller that sellers using internet negative-option features must disclose material terms before collecting billing information, get express informed consent before charging, and provide simple ways to stop recurring charges.

The practical takeaway: disclose early, get consent in writing, keep the amount reasonable. If you coach in a regulated context, such as alongside a license in health care, follow your licensing board’s rules too. If you are unsure, a short review of your agreement by a business attorney in your state is inexpensive protection.

How much should you charge?

There is no official number for coaching. The defensible range is anchored to what the session costs.

Approach How it works Best for
Full session fee Late cancellations and no-shows are charged at the normal rate One-off or pay-per-session clients
Fixed fee A stated flat amount, usually below the session rate Clients on packages, where a per-session price is unclear
Tiered Late cancellation costs part of the fee, a no-show costs the full fee Coaches who want a softer first step
Session forfeited The missed session counts as used in a package Prepaid packages and retainers

Coaching Studies suggests wording where sessions cancelled or rescheduled with less than 24 hours’ notice are late cancellations charged the session fee or a stated fixed fee, and no-shows without prior notice are charged. That 24-hour line is a common starting point, but it is your call. An executive coach with a packed calendar may need 48 hours. A coach with flexible gaps may be fine with 12.

A worked example

Say you charge $250 per session and meet a client twice a month. Your policy says: cancel with 24 hours’ notice or more and reschedule at no charge. Cancel with less, or miss the session, and the session fee applies.

If that client misses one call, you invoice $250, the same as the session. That is easy to explain and well within the range FindLaw describes. If you instead charged $400 for a $250 session, you would invite a dispute you could lose.

Now the prepaid package version. The client bought six sessions for $1,350. Your policy says a missed session without notice is counted as delivered. No invoice is needed, and nobody has to feel punished. The client simply has five sessions left.

Build in a little grace

Many coaches allow one free reschedule per quarter, or waive the fee for real emergencies. A grace rule costs you little and keeps good clients. It also shows that the fee is about respect for the time, not about collecting penalties. Whatever you choose, write it down. An unwritten exception becomes an argument later.

What your policy should say

Keep it short enough that a client will actually read it. Cover these points:

  1. Notice window. How many hours before the session a change must be made, and how (email, portal message, text).
  2. What counts as late and what counts as a no-show. Define both. A client who arrives 20 minutes into a 50-minute session needs a stated rule.
  3. The fee. The exact amount or formula, in dollars.
  4. Rescheduling. Whether on-time changes are free, and how many are allowed.
  5. Exceptions. Illness, emergencies, or your own cancellations.
  6. Your own obligations. If you cancel late, what do you offer the client? Mutual rules feel fair.
  7. Payment. When the fee is due and how it is collected.

Here is sample wording you can adapt:

Sessions are held for you. If you need to change a session, please let me know at least 24 hours before the start time. Sessions cancelled or rescheduled with less notice, and sessions missed without notice, are charged at the full session fee of $[amount]. If I need to reschedule on short notice, I will offer you a replacement time or credit the session. Emergencies are handled case by case.

Have the client agree to it, not merely receive it. The APA’s Ethics Code, Standard 6.04 is written for psychologists, not coaches, but its approach travels well: agree on fees and billing arrangements as early as feasible, keep fees lawful, and tell clients before turning to collection agencies or legal measures. Many coaches never go near collections. They treat the fee as a conversation, then a invoice, then a decision about the relationship.

How to collect a no-show fee

The easiest fee to collect is the one you do not have to chase. A few methods, from smoothest to most awkward:

  • Prepaid packages or monthly retainers. The missed session is already paid for. No invoice, no conversation about money.
  • Payment at booking. The client pays before the session, so a miss is a forfeited session, not a debt.
  • Card on file with written consent. The client agrees in the contract that the fee may be charged to the card. Be exact about what you will charge and when, and keep the signed agreement. Consent matters, as the FTC’s disclosure expectations for recurring charges show.
  • A separate invoice. Send it within a day or two, with the policy section quoted and a friendly note. Do not let weeks pass.

A sample message after a no-show:

Hi Jordan, we missed you today at 2:00. I held the time for you, so per our agreement the session fee of $250 applies. I have attached the invoice. I would love to find a time this week to get back on track. What works for you?

Short, warm, and factual. No apology, no accusation.

When no-shows become a pattern

One missed call is an accident. Three in a row is information. Before you reach for the fee, look at what is actually happening.

Coaching Studies advises addressing the pattern directly and with curiosity, exploring what makes the schedule hard, and considering changes to the timing, frequency, duration or format of sessions. You can also explain that future late cancellations will be charged under your agreement. Here is a simple sequence:

  1. Name it kindly. “I have noticed we have moved the last three sessions. What is going on?”
  2. Find the cause. Is it a calendar problem, a motivation problem, or a sign that the coaching goal has shifted?
  3. Adjust the format. Shorter calls, a phone option instead of video, or booking one session at a time around the client’s availability are all options Coaching Studies mentions.
  4. Restate the policy. Remind the client that the agreement applies from here on.
  5. Decide together. If coaching is no longer a priority, pausing or ending the engagement is a legitimate, respectful outcome.

This order protects the relationship and your income. Most clients respond to the first or second step. The fee is the backstop, not the opening move.

Prevent the miss in the first place

Fees recover cost. Prevention keeps the session on the calendar. The simplest tool is a reminder. A systematic review and meta-analysis of text, call and email reminders found that SMS reminders positively affected appointment attendance, with a relative risk of attendance of 1.09 for adults aged 24 to 40 and for those aged 50 to 63. That is a modest lift, in health care settings, but reminders cost almost nothing.

A practical prevention checklist:

  • Send a reminder 24 hours before each session and a short one the morning of.
  • Put the next session on the calendar before the current one ends.
  • Let clients reschedule themselves in a portal, so changing is easier than ghosting.
  • Give every session a clear purpose and a bit of prep, so skipping feels like losing something.
  • Review attendance monthly and talk about any drift early.

Note that the studies behind reminder effects come from health care, not coaching. Treat them as a reasonable starting point, then track your own numbers.

A coach’s view: policy is part of the container

We built ClickCoach after seeing how much of a coach’s week disappears into administration, and missed sessions are a big part of it. Our view is simple: a no-show policy is not a punishment tool. It is part of the container that makes coaching work.

When the agreement, the schedule, the notes and the billing all live in one place, the policy stops being a document that nobody opens. The client signs it, sees their next session in the portal, and knows what happens if they cannot make it. You see the pattern in your session history instead of relying on memory. That makes the pattern conversation concrete: you can say “we have rescheduled three of the last five sessions” and mean it.

Our advice to coaches: write the policy once, keep it short, apply it consistently, and waive it deliberately rather than by accident. Consistency is what clients remember. A coach who enforces a policy kindly earns more respect than one who never mentions it and quietly resents every miss.

A short implementation checklist

Use this to go from no policy to a working one in an afternoon:

  1. Decide your notice window (24 or 48 hours is typical).
  2. Choose the fee model: full fee, fixed fee, or forfeited session.
  3. Check the amount against your session price so it stays reasonable.
  4. Write the policy in plain language, under one page.
  5. Add it to your coaching agreement and get a signature or clear written consent.
  6. Decide your exceptions and your own late-cancel remedy.
  7. Set up reminders and an easy way for clients to reschedule.
  8. Draft the message you will send after a no-show, so you are not improvising.
  9. Review attendance every month.
  10. Have a business attorney in your state glance over the final version.

If you already have clients, introduce the change with notice. Tell them what is changing, why, and when it starts, and let them confirm. Do not apply a new fee to a miss that happened before they agreed to it.

FAQ

Is it legal to charge a no-show fee for coaching?

Generally yes, when the fee is reasonable and disclosed before the client books. Unusually high fees can be unenforceable, and some states regulate specific kinds of instruction contracts. Have an attorney in your state review your agreement.

How much should I charge for a missed coaching session?

Most coaches charge the regular session fee or a stated fixed fee at or below it. Keep the amount tied to the cost of the session, since a charge well above the service price is the kind most likely to be challenged. Write the exact dollar figure into your agreement.

What notice should I require for cancellations?

24 hours is a common standard, and some coaches use 48. Pick a window that fits how hard your slots are to refill, and apply the same window to yourself when you cancel.

Should I charge the first time a client misses?

That is your decision, and many coaches offer a one-time courtesy waiver. If you do, say so in the policy or the message so it reads as a deliberate gesture. For repeated misses, talk with the client first, then apply the fee as the agreement states.

Do reminders really reduce no-shows?

Research in health care settings found that SMS reminders improve attendance modestly. Reminders are cheap and low-risk, so they are worth using alongside a policy. Track your own attendance to see what they do for your clients.
