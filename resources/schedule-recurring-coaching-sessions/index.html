How to Schedule Recurring Coaching Sessions That Hold

Set up recurring coaching sessions that stay on the calendar: cadence, series length, calendar sync, reminders, cancellation rules and time zones, step by step.

By Mitch Russo · Updated 2026-10-27

To schedule recurring coaching sessions, pick a cadence and a series length with your client, book the whole series at once at a fixed day and time, connect it to the calendar you actually live in, and write down what happens when a session gets moved or missed. Do those four things before the first session and most scheduling problems never show up.

This guide walks through each step, with the settings to check in common calendar tools, a cancellation policy you can adapt, and what changes when you coach a group or a team across time zones.

TL;DR

  • Book the series, not the single session. A fixed weekly, bi-weekly or monthly slot protects the client’s momentum and your own calendar.
  • Make sure your booking tool can see far enough ahead. A six-week weekly series needs at least 42 days of open future availability.
  • Connect every calendar you use so booked times block each other, and choose the reminder timing in advance.
  • Decide in writing how “this session only” and “this and all future sessions” changes work, plus your late-cancellation window.
  • For groups, anchor the series to one time zone and publish it with the local time for each member.

Start with cadence and series length

Before you open any tool, decide two numbers with the client: how often you meet and how many sessions the series covers.

Most coaching engagements land on one of three rhythms: weekly, every two weeks, or monthly. Weekly suits a fast start, a launch, or a client who needs accountability. Bi-weekly gives time to complete action steps between sessions. Monthly works for senior leaders with demanding calendars or for maintenance phases after a main engagement. Scheduling tools reflect the same pattern. BetterUp’s support page, for example, lists monthly, bi-weekly or weekly sessions for its recurring feature, where available, and limits booking to six months in advance.

That last point matters for you too. Every tool has a ceiling on how far ahead it will book, and a series that runs longer than the ceiling quietly stops creating sessions.

Match the series to the engagement

A practical rule: book the series to match the contract, not the calendar year.

Engagement Typical cadence Series to book
Three-month sprint Weekly 12 sessions
Six-month executive engagement Bi-weekly 12 sessions
Ongoing support after the main program Monthly 3 to 6 sessions, then renew
Pilot or trial Weekly 4 sessions, then decide

These are working patterns, not industry standards. Adjust them to your method. The point is that the client sees a defined start and end, which makes renewal a conversation about results rather than an awkward invoice.

Leave room for a renewal checkpoint

Book the last session of every series as a review. Look at the goals you set at the start, what changed, and what comes next. If you book a series of 12, make session 11 or 12 the checkpoint so the renewal decision happens while the series is still running, not after it lapses.

Set up a recurring schedule step by step

The mechanics differ by tool, but the sequence is the same everywhere.

  1. Agree the slot live. Choose a day and time you can both protect for the whole series. Ask the client what usually derails that slot, such as a standing staff meeting or school pickup.
  2. Create the series with an interval and an end. Set the repeat pattern (weekly, every two weeks, monthly) and either an end date or a maximum number of sessions. An open-ended series is hard to renew and hard to end.
  3. Check the booking window. FluentBooking’s documentation says a recurring series needs an interval and a maximum number of events, and that the calendar must allow bookings far enough into the future to cover the whole series. Its example: a six-week series needs at least 42 days of future availability. If your window is 30 days, the last sessions will not book.
  4. Add the meeting link and agenda to the invite. Use one standing video link for the whole series so the client never hunts for a new URL.
  5. Send a confirmation that lists every date. Clients lose track of single calendar entries. A short message with all session dates, the time zone, and your change policy removes most confusion.
  6. Put the renewal checkpoint on the calendar now. See above.

If you are choosing a booking tool, it helps to think about trust as much as features. Clients who can book, see and change sessions without friction feel looked after, which is part of building client trust when you work remotely.

Pick a tool for the way you work

A standalone scheduler is fine for a handful of clients. Look for these features, which coaches in the Reddit r/Coaching thread on scheduling software list as must-haves: two-way calendar sync, buffer times before and after meetings, and the ability to schedule recurring meetings. Not every popular scheduler offers true recurring booking, so test it before you promise clients a series. Book a fake three-session series with a friend and confirm that all three appear on both calendars.

Sync your calendar and prevent double bookings

A recurring series fails most often when it collides with something else. Fix that at the source.

If you use Google Calendar, Google’s help page explains how to create an appointment schedule: set the schedule’s time zone, then choose which calendars to check for availability. Google states that schedules and booked appointments sync automatically with your calendar. With “Check calendars for availability” selected, bookable times do not appear during events on the chosen calendars, including events you responded Yes or Maybe to.

Three habits make this reliable:

  • Connect every calendar. Personal, family and other-job calendars all count. A dentist appointment on a calendar your scheduler cannot see is a double booking waiting to happen.
  • Add buffers. Ten to fifteen minutes after each session gives you time to write notes while they are fresh. Notes taken the same day are more accurate and take less effort.
  • Block your own non-negotiables. Admin time, a weekly review, lunch. If it is not on the calendar, a client can book over it.

One caution: availability checks apply to what is bookable. A series you create manually by duplicating events may bypass those checks, so scan the first few weeks by eye after setup.

Reminders and session details clients should receive

A reminder should do two jobs: confirm the time and remove friction. Whatever tool you use, each recurring session should carry the date and time in the client’s time zone, the video link or location, and a one-line prompt such as “Bring your top two wins and one obstacle from this week.”

Check what your tool actually sends. Google says eligible Google Workspace and Google One plans can send up to five reminders before each appointment, and the reminder email text cannot be edited. That means a generic reminder, so your personal prompt needs to live somewhere else, such as the invite description or a message from you. For comparison, BetterUp sends an email when a session is created, plus an email and an SMS 10 minutes before, subject to program details. That is a useful benchmark: a confirmation at booking and a last-minute nudge.

A simple reminder rhythm for private clients:

  • At booking: confirmation with all dates.
  • 24 hours before: reminder with the agenda prompt and the link.
  • 1 hour before or 10 minutes before: short nudge, only if your client likes it.

Use the between-session gap

The reminder that matters most may be the one that is not about the session. A midweek check-in on the homework you assigned keeps the series moving. Keep it short and specific to the action step, not a generic “how’s it going.”

Handle holidays, cancellations and reschedules

The policy is the part coaches skip and later regret. Write it in the agreement and repeat it in the confirmation message.

Separate “one session” from “the series”

Good tools distinguish between moving one occurrence and changing everything. BetterUp’s U.S. support documentation shows the pattern: after you select a recurring session and choose reschedule, you pick either “This session only” or “This and all future sessions”, then choose a new available time. Know which option you are clicking. Choosing the wrong one is how a one-off conflict wipes out a standing slot.

Set a late-change window

The same BetterUp page defines a cancellation or reschedule less than 24 hours before the start as late, and counts it toward program usage for time-limited programs. It also removes subsequent recurring sessions after three missed sessions in a row. You are not a platform, but the structure is worth borrowing: a clear window, a clear consequence, and a clear point at which you check in rather than keep silently rebooking.

A policy you can adapt:

  • Changes with 24 hours or more of notice are rescheduled at no charge, within the same week when possible.
  • Changes inside 24 hours count as a delivered session, unless you decide to make an exception.
  • After two missed sessions in a row, you contact the client personally to decide whether to pause, restructure or end the series.

Exceptions are your call. A client’s family emergency deserves flexibility. What matters is that exceptions are exceptions.

Plan for holidays up front

U.S. clients often travel around federal holidays and school breaks. When you build the series, look at the next six months and skip the weeks you know will not work, such as the week of Thanksgiving or the end of December, instead of cancelling them one by one. Tell the client the series will take 14 calendar weeks to deliver 12 sessions, and the skipped weeks stop being a surprise.

Recurring group and team sessions across time zones

Groups add a harder problem: no single time suits everyone. Meetergo’s guide to group coaching names scheduling across time zones as one of the first challenges of running group sessions, alongside registration, payment and no-shows.

What works in practice:

  • Anchor the series to one time zone. Publish “Thursdays at 12:00 p.m. Eastern” and let calendar invites convert it for each member. Do not publish times in three zones by hand.
  • Send the invite, not just the text. A calendar invite adjusts automatically; a line in an email does not.
  • Watch the daylight saving shift. The U.S. changes clocks on different dates than much of Europe, so a global group can see its meeting move an hour for a few weeks. Mention it in the kickoff message.
  • Rotate or record. If your cohort spans very distant zones, offer two session times or a recording with a short written summary. Pick one primary and one fallback and keep both fixed.
  • Set attendance expectations. Decide how many sessions a member can miss and still complete the program.

For a team engagement, ask the sponsor for the participants’ work locations before proposing a time. A series that is fine for the HR lead may fall at 6 a.m. for someone on the West Coast.

Our perspective: scheduling is part of the method

We built ClickCoach because scheduling rarely fails on its own. It fails because the session, the notes, the action plan and the follow-up live in different places. When the calendar says Tuesday at 10 but the notes from last Tuesday are in another app and the homework is in an email thread, the coach spends the first ten minutes reconstructing the story.

Our view is that a recurring session is a unit of the client’s progress. Each occurrence should connect to the goals you set, the commitments the client made, and what you will review next. A one-page recurring session checklist

Copy this into your onboarding template.

  1. Cadence and series length agreed (and written in the agreement).
  2. Day, time and time zone confirmed.
  3. Booking window covers the whole series.
  4. All personal and work calendars connected; buffers added.
  5. Standing video link in every invite.
  6. Confirmation sent listing every date.
  7. Reminder rhythm chosen; agenda prompt included.
  8. Late-change window and missed-session rule stated.
  9. Known holidays skipped on purpose.
  10. Renewal checkpoint booked.

Track two numbers each month: sessions delivered versus sessions booked, and the share of series that renewed. They tell you quickly whether your system or your offer needs attention.

FAQ

How far ahead should I book a recurring coaching series?

Book the whole contracted series if your tool allows it, with a renewal checkpoint near the end. Check your tool’s limits first: BetterUp’s recurring feature, for example, limits booking to six months ahead. If your series is longer, book in phases and tell the client when the next block will be added.

What is the best cadence for coaching sessions?

It depends on the client’s goals and the pace of their action steps. Weekly suits early momentum, bi-weekly leaves room to complete work between sessions, and monthly fits maintenance or senior clients with tight calendars. Start with the cadence in your method and adjust after the first month.

How do I stop double bookings?

Connect every calendar you use to your scheduler and turn on the setting that checks those calendars for conflicts. In Google Calendar, that is the option to check calendars for availability when you create an appointment schedule. Add buffers between sessions and review the first few weeks of any new series by eye.

Should I charge for late cancellations?

Many coaches define a window, often 24 hours, and count late changes as delivered sessions. Write the rule in your agreement and apply it consistently, with room for genuine emergencies. Be clear about what happens after repeated misses so you can check in rather than keep rebooking.
