How to Send Coaching Newsletters Clients Read

A practical system for coaches who want to send coaching newsletters: what to include, how often, legal basics, deliverability, and what to measure.

By Mitch Russo · Updated 2026-10-23

To send a coaching newsletter that works, pick one audience, promise one clear benefit, and mail on a schedule you can keep for a year. Then add the legal footer, set up sender authentication, and track replies and booked calls instead of open rates.

That is the whole system. The rest of this guide shows you how to build it in a week, with templates, checklists and the numbers worth watching.

I wrote it for working coaches: executive, life, business and group coaches who want a steady line to prospects and past clients without adding hours of admin to the week.

TL;DR

  • Choose one reader and one promise. A newsletter that tries to serve every client type serves none of them.
  • Pick a cadence you can sustain (weekly, biweekly or monthly) and keep to it.
  • Every marketing email needs a physical postal address, an honest sender line and a working unsubscribe, under the CAN-SPAM Act.
  • Judge success by replies, booked calls and conversions, not open rates alone.
  • Keep client-specific messages (session recaps, homework) separate from marketing newsletters.

Decide what your newsletter is for

Before you write a subject line, write one sentence: “This newsletter helps [specific person] do [specific thing].”

For example: “Helps new engineering managers run better one-on-ones.” Or: “Helps burned-out professionals rebuild their weekends.” If you cannot finish the sentence, you are not ready to send yet.

A coaching newsletter usually has one of three jobs:

  1. Attract. Bring cold subscribers into your world and toward a discovery call.
  2. Nurture. Keep past prospects warm until the timing is right for them.
  3. Retain. Keep current and former clients engaged, so renewals and referrals come naturally.

Pick one as the primary job. You can serve a second one, but the first decides what you write and what you ask readers to do.

Who should be on the list

Send only to people who have a real relationship with you or who opted in. That means:

  • Visitors who signed up through a form on your site.
  • Discovery-call attendees who agreed to hear from you.
  • Past and current clients.
  • Workshop and webinar attendees who opted in.

Do not import a purchased list or add everyone from a networking event. Beyond the legal risk, it wrecks your sender reputation fast.

Start with two or three segments at most. Constant Contact’s segmentation guidance warns against slicing a list into slivers you cannot serve consistently, and recommends beginning with clear, high-impact criteria such as lifecycle stage or niche. For most coaches that looks like this:

Segment Who What they get
Prospects Subscribers who have not booked a call Educational pieces, one soft invitation per issue
Active clients People currently in a program Not the marketing newsletter: session-specific messages instead
Alumni Past clients Updates, referral asks, new program announcements

Notice that active clients are not on the marketing newsletter by default. More on that below.

Choose a format and a cadence you can keep

The best cadence is the one you will still be using in month twelve. Consistency builds trust faster than volume.

  • Weekly suits coaches who like writing and have a prospect-heavy list.
  • Biweekly is a good default for solo practitioners with a full client load.
  • Monthly works for alumni lists and executive audiences who value fewer, denser messages.

Then choose a repeatable structure so each issue takes under an hour. Here is one that works well:

  1. One idea. A single insight, story or framework, 250 to 400 words.
  2. One practice. A question or exercise the reader can try this week.
  3. One invitation. A single call to action: reply, read, or book a call.

First-person essays are common among coaches who write well, and that is fine. What matters is that every issue has a point and one next step. Litmus’s newsletter guide describes newsletters as recurring roundups of tips, news or updates, which is a useful reminder that recurring and predictable is the point.

Ten topics that fill a quarter

If you wonder what to say, work from your sessions. Without naming anyone or sharing confidential details, you already know what your clients struggle with.

  1. The most common mistake you see in your niche, and the fix.
  2. A framework you use in sessions, explained in plain words.
  3. A question you ask that changes the conversation.
  4. A short story of a pattern (anonymized and composite, never a real client without written permission).
  5. A tool or book you recommend, and why.
  6. A myth about your kind of coaching, corrected.
  7. A weekly or monthly reflection exercise.
  8. A behind-the-scenes note on how you run your method.
  9. A reader question you answer in depth.
  10. A program announcement with the specific problem it solves.

Write the issue

Subject line and preview

Be specific and honest. A subject line that names the benefit beats a clever one, and a deceptive subject line is also prohibited by the CAN-SPAM Act. Write the preview text so it adds information instead of repeating the subject.

The body

Write as you speak in a session: short paragraphs, plain words, one idea. Use “you” often. End with a question the reader can answer by hitting reply. Replies tell inbox providers that people want your mail, and they start conversations that lead to clients.

The call to action

One ask per issue. If you want people to book a call, do not also ask them to buy a course, join a webinar and follow you on three platforms. Make the next step so clear that a tired reader on a phone can take it.

Keep it legal: CAN-SPAM basics for coaches

If you are in the United States or send to US subscribers, the rules apply to every commercial message, including a newsletter that promotes your services. The FTC’s compliance guide lays out the requirements. In short, your emails must:

  • Use accurate “From,” “To” and routing information, so the sender is identifiable.
  • Avoid deceptive subject lines.
  • Identify the message as an ad when it is one.
  • Include your valid physical postal address.
  • Give recipients a clear way to opt out, and honor opt-outs promptly. The FTC says you have 10 business days.

The penalties are real: the FTC guide lists fines of up to $53,088 per violating email, and that figure is adjusted over time, so check the current number on the FTC page before you cite it.

One distinction matters a lot for coaches. The FTC treats transactional or relationship messages, those that facilitate an agreed transaction, differently from commercial ones. A session confirmation or an invoice reminder is a service message. A newsletter promoting your new group program is marketing. Keep the two apart in your tools and your head, and put the unsubscribe and address in anything promotional.

If you coach clients outside the US, such as in the EU or the UK, other rules on consent apply. Ask a qualified attorney familiar with those jurisdictions before mailing there.

Make sure your emails reach the inbox

Good writing does not matter if the message lands in spam. Three things protect you.

Authenticate your domain

Google’s sender guidelines ask all senders to authenticate outgoing mail. Senders of 5,000 or more messages a day to Gmail accounts face the strictest list: authentication, easy unsubscribes and low spam complaints. Most solo coaches are nowhere near that volume, but the same practices still protect small senders, and your email platform will walk you through setting up SPF and DKIM on your domain. Do this once, before the first send.

Watch spam complaints

The same Google guidelines advise keeping user-reported spam rates low, with 0.3% as the line you should never reach. On a list of 1,000, that is three people hitting “report spam” per send. Make unsubscribing easy so people leave through the link, not the spam button.

Clean your list

Once a quarter, remove or pause contacts who have not engaged for a long stretch. Constant Contact notes that pruning inactive contacts protects your sender reputation. A smaller, engaged list beats a large, cold one.

Measure what matters

Open rates have become less reliable because of Apple’s Mail Privacy Protection. Litmus reports that email marketers are shifting toward impact metrics, such as subscriber-to-customer conversion and how long a new subscriber takes to take a first action.

For a coaching practice, a simple scorecard is enough:

Metric Why it matters How to track it
Replies per issue Shows real engagement Count them in your inbox
Discovery calls booked from email Ties the newsletter to revenue Use a dedicated booking link
Subscriber-to-client rate The ultimate result Divide new clients from email by new subscribers over a quarter
Unsubscribe rate Flags a content mismatch Your email platform’s report
Spam complaints Protects deliverability Your email platform’s report

For context on unsubscribes, Mailchimp’s benchmarks put the average across industries at roughly 0.15% to 0.22%. If you are well above that, reread your last five issues and ask whether the content matches what people signed up for. Also check frequency.

Review the scorecard monthly and change one thing at a time: a subject-line style, a send time, or the call to action.

Automate the routine, keep the human parts

A newsletter is one piece of a larger flow. Constant Contact’s strategy guide recommends using automation for key moments such as welcome series and post-session follow-ups, while still testing subject lines and send times. Automation should free up time for personal attention, not replace it.

A practical sequence for a new subscriber:

  1. Day 0: a welcome email that delivers the promised lead magnet and says what to expect and when.
  2. Day 3: your best piece of content on their core problem.
  3. Day 7: a short story about how you work, ending with an invitation to a discovery call.
  4. Then: they join the regular newsletter.

Newsletters for prospects, messages for clients

This is where many coaches blur lines. Your newsletter is marketing. What you send your current clients between sessions is part of the coaching itself: a recap, the agreed action steps, a reminder of this week’s homework.

Mixing them causes problems. Clients may feel they are being sold to, and a promotional blast can bury a message that matters to their progress. Keeping them separate also keeps you clear on which messages carry the commercial-email requirements.

Our perspective: protect the client relationship first

We talk to coaches every week, and the same pattern keeps showing up. They spend Sunday nights stitching together a newsletter app, a scheduler, a notes document, an invoicing tool and a spreadsheet of client goals. Then they wonder why they have no time to coach or to write.

Our view is simple. A newsletter is worth sending when it grows out of the real work you do with clients. The best ideas come from patterns in sessions, and the best proof comes from documented progress. If your session notes, goals and results live in one place, writing an honest issue takes less effort, and you can speak about outcomes without relying on memory. (Always anonymize, and never share client details without written consent.)

We also think coaches should keep marketing and client communication in separate lanes, and keep both inside a workflow they can sustain. A small, consistent newsletter plus excellent follow-up with current clients will outperform an ambitious content calendar that collapses in March.

A one-week launch checklist

  1. Monday: write your one-sentence promise and choose your cadence.
  2. Tuesday: pick an email platform, add your postal address and set up your sender domain (SPF and DKIM).
  3. Wednesday: build a signup form and a short welcome email.
  4. Thursday: draft issue one using the idea, practice, invitation structure.
  5. Friday: send a test to yourself on desktop and phone. Check the unsubscribe link, the address in the footer and the reply-to.
  6. Weekend: schedule the send and put the next four issue topics on your calendar.

After a month, review the scorecard above. After a quarter, clean the list.

FAQ

How often should a coach send a newsletter?

As often as you can keep up for a year. Biweekly is a solid default for solo coaches with full client loads, weekly suits prospect-heavy lists, and monthly works for alumni. Watch your unsubscribe rate and replies, and adjust if either moves the wrong way.

Do I need a physical address in my coaching emails?

Yes, if the email is commercial. The FTC requires a valid physical postal address in marketing emails, along with a clear way to opt out. A post office box or a private mailbox registered with a commercial mail receiving agency can work, so you do not have to publish your home address. Confirm the details with an attorney if you are unsure.

Can I add my current clients to my newsletter?

It is better to keep active clients out of the marketing newsletter by default and ask first. Session recaps and homework reminders are part of your service and should go through your client portal or direct messages. Invite clients to the newsletter if they would like it, and always include an unsubscribe.

What is a good open rate for a coaching newsletter?

There is no reliable single number, because Apple’s Mail Privacy Protection inflates opens. Litmus notes the shift toward impact metrics. Track replies, booked calls and how many subscribers become clients instead.
